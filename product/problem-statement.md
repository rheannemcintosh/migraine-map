# Problem Statement

## The Challenge

### Who this is for

People in the UK who live with migraine and are under the care of a neurologist, headache specialist, or specialist nurse, and who are required to keep a migraine diary as a condition of accessing and trialling treatments. This is a large population: migraine affects almost 10 million people in the UK, around 1 in 7, and roughly 1 million live with chronic migraine, meaning headache on at least 15 days a month [1]. It falls disproportionately on women, who are affected at roughly twice the rate of men [1]. Those with severe or complex migraine are referred to specialists, where keeping an accurate diary becomes essential to being assessed for and kept on treatments such as Botox, nerve blocks, infusions, and the various preventives trialled over months and years.

### The problem

For patients under specialist care, the migraine diary is not optional. It is the clinical record used to decide whether a treatment is working and whether the patient continues to qualify for it. Yet the way this record is kept fails both the patient keeping it and the clinician relying on it.

**It is kept on paper, and paper is easy to fail.** Paper is the standard diary format issued to patients: the major UK migraine bodies and individual NHS clinics all distribute printable paper templates as the default way to track attacks, and specialist diagnosis is described as relying on paper diaries that are acknowledged to be unreliable [3][4]. Digital tools do exist, but the form a patient is typically handed is paper. A patient is expected to record a score every single day, often for years on end, but does not carry the paper everywhere, so days get missed. A migraine can come and go across a day and then has to be scored in retrospect, from memory, which is unreliable. In practice, patients end up scattering notes across their phone, work laptop, and paper, then compiling it all the night before an appointment, which defeats the point of a daily record.

**The scores are subjective and inconsistent.** Diaries typically ask for a single number, but the scale is arbitrary and hard to apply. It is genuinely difficult to tell whether a given attack is a 4 or a 7, and there is no shared reference for what those numbers mean, even for the same person from one day to the next. The scale also differs between providers. The same patient moving from NHS Scotland to a London hospital can go from a 0 to 3 scale to a 0 to 10 scale, with no way to translate their own history across the two, so continuity of their record is lost at exactly the point continuity matters most.

**The data is then counted by hand.** Specialist nurses report having to manually tally migraine days and add up scores from these paper diaries. This is slow, and it introduces counting errors into the very numbers that treatment decisions rest on. At a system level, the absence of a consistent, meaningful migraine dataset has long been recognised as a barrier to planning and evaluating services [2].

### Why existing options fall short

Paper diaries are the standard format patients are given [3][4], and they carry every problem above: easy to forget, impossible to keep on hand at all times, retrospective, and unreadable as data without manual counting. Generic note-taking apps and phone notes are what patients reach for instead, but they are unstructured, do not match the specific scale a clinic requires, and still have to be manually compiled and transcribed before an appointment. Neither approach gives the patient a record that is simple to keep consistently, nor gives the clinician one that is reliable to read.

### What it costs

The cost is borne on both sides. For the patient, an incomplete or inaccurate diary can mean being assessed as not responding to a treatment, or being unable to demonstrate response, which can affect whether they stay on it. For the clinician and the NHS, decisions about expensive, limited treatments are being made on hand-counted data of uncertain accuracy. This sits inside a system already under acute strain: average waits to see a migraine specialist nearly doubled from 15 weeks in 2021 to 29 weeks in 2023 [1], headache and migraine account for around a third of new neurology referrals [1][2], and the UK has only 1.1 full-time-equivalent neurologists per 100,000 people, against 4 per 100,000 in France and Germany [1]. When specialist time is this scarce, the quality of the record a patient brings to it matters enormously, and right now that record is the weakest link.

### Author's Note

This problem statement is grounded in first-hand experience of living with migraine for nearly 17 years, since age 13, and being treated under both NHS Scotland and a specialist centre in England, including keeping diaries under two different scoring systems (0 to 3 and 0 to 10) across nearly every NHS-available treatment, and in direct accounts from NHS specialist nurses about manual counting of paper diaries.
